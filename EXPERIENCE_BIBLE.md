# EXPERIENCE BIBLE

*The Heart says why we exist. The Manifesto says how the product must behave. This says how every moment must feel.*

*Designers, writers, voice actors, developers, content producers: this is the document you defend your work with. It contains no screens, no components, no patterns. It will still be true when the application has been rebuilt three times.*

*Our design material is not pixels. It is human attention, which is finite, involuntary, and easily stolen — most often by us.*

---

## 1. THE EXPERIENCE WE DESIGN

Not tourism. Not education. Not a game.

We design **the experience of being let in on something.**

The closest existing form is not a tour and not a museum. It is the friend who knows a country impossibly well, who takes you somewhere on a Tuesday, who tells you one thing while you are standing there, and who does not talk for the rest of the walk.

Three ingredients make that feeling, and all three must be present:

**Privilege.** Few people know this. You now do.
**Intimacy.** It was told to you, not announced to everyone. Someone thought of you.
**Proof.** You are standing on it. It cannot be argued with.

A museum gives everyone the same thing in a room. A guidebook gives everyone the same thing in advance. A friend gives one person one thing, in the place, at the right moment — and that is the register we work in, at scale, without ever letting it feel like scale.

One more property. The experience must feel **found, not produced.** Everything we make should carry the sense that it existed before us and would survive without us. The instant a person can feel authorship, the place becomes an exhibit and they become an audience.

---

## 2. THE EMOTIONAL ARC

From *"I have no plans"* to *"I am standing there."* Thirteen states. No screens.

**1. Idleness.** Sunday evening. Unformed restlessness. Not searching for anything. This is the hardest state to enter, because there is no intent to attach to.

**2. The snag.** One fact catches. Involuntary, physical, pre-verbal — the mind simply stops. Not *interest*. A snag.

**3. Disbelief.** *"That can't be near me."* They check. The distance is the second hook and often the stronger one.

**4. Ownership.** The idea stops being a suggestion and becomes theirs. Everything before this is ours. Everything after is theirs. This is the most important transition in the entire product and it is almost never designed.

**5. Declaration.** They say it out loud to another person. *"We should go Saturday."* Point of no return — and it happens away from any screen, which is exactly why it must be planned for.

**6. Logistics.** Weather, hours, who is driving, what time. Unglamorous. **This is where journeys die.** More trips are lost here than anywhere else, and it is the least designed stage in every travel product ever built.

**7. Anticipation.** The night before. Rare, precious, almost universally wasted.

**8. Transit.** Boredom with a rising floor underneath it.

**9. Threshold.** The last kilometres. The road narrows, the signs stop. Doubt appears: *are we lost?*

**10. Awkwardness.** Arrival. Stiff from the car, bags, parking, orientation. Nobody feels awe here.

**11. Landing.** The reversal. The reason for all of it.

**12. Afterglow.** The walk back to the car. Quiet, slightly stunned, hungry.

**13. Retelling.** Hours to weeks later, repeatedly, to different people.

The two conclusions worth pinning to a wall: **our highest-leverage moment is state 4, and our most neglected risk is state 6.** Almost everyone in this industry designs states 10 and 11 and loses the person at state 6.

---

## 3. THE RHYTHM

The experience breathes. Expansion, then contraction. Never a constant.

**Fast** — discovery and decision. Astonishment must arrive before doubt does. Nobody should ever wait to be surprised.

**Slow** — everything on site. Slowness is a claim about worth, and it must be earned by the place, never applied as a default mood.

**Quiet** — the last stretch of road, the first minute of arrival, the moment, the first miles home.

**Surprising** — once per place. Exactly once. Surprise is a currency and we are the only ones who can debase it.

**Reflective** — the drive home, and years later when a memory is reopened.

**Urgent** — almost never, and only when the world is genuinely urgent: the light is going, the gate closes at six, the season ends in a fortnight. Real urgency serves the person. Manufactured urgency serves us, and everyone can tell the difference.

**Playful** — planning, children, the middle of a walk. Never at the moment. Never near a grave.

**Sacred** — rare, earned, and shorter than instinct suggests. The sacred register holds for about ninety seconds before it curdles into theatre.

The master rule of rhythm: **the product speaks less as the place speaks more.** Our volume is inversely proportional to the significance of what is in front of the person.

---

## 4. THE FIRST TEN SECONDS

Emotionally, not visually.

The target state at second ten is **mild disbelief about a fact** — not admiration of a product.

What must happen: the person feels *addressed*. Something specific was set aside for them. Within ten seconds there should be one small involuntary internal noise — *huh* — and it should be about the world, not about us.

What must not happen: being welcomed. Being oriented. Being asked anything. Being introduced to a brand. Being made to understand a concept. Being impressed.

It should feel like **opening a door onto something already in progress** — not like launching something that has been waiting for you.

And they should not yet know what this is. That is fine. Confusion is survivable; indifference is not. Curiosity outperforms comprehension every time in the first ten seconds.

---

## 5. THE FIRST FIVE MINUTES

**What disappears:** the evaluative posture. Within five minutes the person should have stopped asking *is this any good* and started asking *is that real*. Also gone: the self-consciousness of being marketed to, and the sense of being a user of something.

**What becomes important:** one place, one distance, one possible date. Nothing else has been introduced and nothing else needs to be.

**What forms:** the impulse to say it out loud. The truest signal in the entire first session is a person turning to someone in the room and starting a sentence — or feeling, for a second, the absence of anyone to say it to.

The emotional deliverable of minute five is a sentence the person can now complete without help:

> *"There's a place near ___ where ___."*

If they cannot say that, the five minutes failed, no matter how good they felt.

---

## 6. THE DRIVE

The great unclaimed territory. Three movements.

**Departure — the first fifth.** Energy, admin, snacks, the argument about music. Nothing we say lands here. **Silence.**

**The long middle.** Anticipation is built here, and it is built *by the people in the car*, not by us. Our job is not to narrate — it is to give the car something to talk about. A question with no clean answer. An unfinished fact. Something a passenger will read out loud unprompted.

The passenger is the most important person in the vehicle and the least designed-for human in travel products. Everything given to the car should be shaped for someone reading it aloud to a driver who cannot look.

**The approach — the last ten to fifteen minutes.** Every other product ramps up here. **We ramp down.** The road narrows, the signs disappear, and we go quiet — because mystery is manufactured by subtraction, and because whatever we say now cannot be said again at the place.

**Should narration begin in the car? No.** Story belongs to the ground. A story spent in a moving vehicle is gone forever and the place inherits an audience that already knows. The car gets context and questions. The place gets story.

**The last kilometre.** One job only: reassurance. *Yes. This road. Keep going.* Doubt at the threshold turns more cars around than distance ever has, and it is invisible in every analytics system ever built.

---

## 7. THE ARRIVAL

The most important chapter in this document. Read it before designing anything that happens on site.

Begin with the truth everyone designs against: **nobody arrives and feels awe.** They arrive and feel *awkward*. Stiff, mildly anxious about the car, unsure where to walk, checking whether anyone needs the toilet. Designing wonder into second five is a fantasy that has ruined a thousand arrivals.

**Seconds 0–30 — the practical fog.**
The person is not available. Doors, bags, orientation, the lock. Our entire job: confirm they are in the right place and answer *where do I walk*. One sentence. No story, no atmosphere, no reward, no sound. Anything else is competing with a car door and losing.

**Seconds 30–90 — the walk in.**
They are moving toward it. We say **nothing.** This is the most important silence in the product. The place is already working — the sound is changing, the temperature is changing, the light is changing — and every one of those signals is more powerful than any sentence we could write. Narration here is theft.

**The threshold.**
Every good place has a physical point where it becomes fully visible, or where it closes around you. We must know exactly where that point is for every location we ever publish, to within a few metres.

At that point: **still nothing.**

A person's first unmediated look belongs to them. Narrate it and we have overwritten their experience with our sentence permanently — for the rest of their life they will remember what we said instead of what they saw.

**The gap — thirty to ninety seconds after the first look.**
Wait. Let them look, turn, adjust, take the obligatory photograph, run out of things to do. Let them get slightly bored. **Boredom at a great place is not a failure — it is the precondition of attention.** The eyes finish their sweep, the mind loosens, and precisely then a question forms on its own: *what is this?*

That question is the door. We do not knock before it opens.

**The first words.**
Quiet, few, and pointed at something they can see from where they are standing. The first sentence must not be beautiful — it must be **specific.** Beauty in the opening line is us performing. Specificity is us pointing.

**Technology arrives last.** Any layer that changes what a person sees — a camera view, an overlay, a reconstruction — comes after they have met the place with their own eyes and formed a relationship with it. Shown first, it replaces the place. Shown last, it deepens it. Nothing mediates a first look, ever.

**Never at arrival:** a reward, a confirmation flourish, a counter, a sound, a vibration, a request, a notification. Arrival is not an achievement. It is a threshold, and thresholds are crossed quietly.

---

## 8. THE PLACE

**Information:** less than they can absorb. One idea per stop. Three to five stops at most. The temptation to add a sixth is the beginning of a lecture.

**Mystery:** every person should leave with one unanswered question. A visitor who leaves fully informed has *closed* the place. A visitor who leaves with a question keeps it — and will come back, or read about it, or tell someone in the form of a question, which is the most contagious form there is.

**Walking:** enough that the body registers where it is. A place absorbed without movement is a photograph you happened to stand inside.

**Freedom:** total. Order is a suggestion. Nothing can be failed, nothing can be missed in a way we announce. If someone abandons everything and sits on a rock for forty minutes, that is a complete and successful visit and we should be glad.

**Our presence:** minimal. Across an entire visit, the product should be active for well under a quarter of the time. If a person is looking at a screen more than at the place, the visit has not been enhanced — it has been replaced.

And visits break. Children, weather, a phone call, a dog, a change of mind. Every experience must survive interruption without penalty, because real life is the interrupting force and we will never win a fight against it.

---

## 9. THE MOMENT

Every place must contain exactly one unforgettable moment, and the moment is **physical, not informational.**

Not a fact landing. Something the body does: looking up, turning around, stepping into a space, noticing that a sound has stopped, seeing what was behind them all along.

It is always specific to a spot. The formula never varies:

> **The right spot + the right direction + the right time + one sentence + then nothing.**

The "then nothing" *is* the moment. Everything before it is scaffolding for a silence.

Our contribution is smaller than it feels: we did not create it. We made sure a person was standing in the correct place, facing the correct way, at an hour when the light works, holding the single piece of knowledge that makes what they are seeing legible. Then we left.

**We never announce a moment in advance.** *"You are about to experience…"* converts revelation into a scheduled event and kills it on contact. Anticipation is built for the place. The moment itself must arrive unannounced.

**What may never interrupt it:** any sound we make. Any vibration. Any notification, from us or anyone. Any request for input. Any suggestion of what comes next. Any signal that it is being recorded or observed. The person must be alone with it even when they are surrounded by their family.

**The moment holds absolute priority over every other need this organisation has.** Commercial, operational, analytical — all of it yields. There is no exception and there will never be a good enough reason.

---

## 10. THE RETURN

Nobody designs the drive home. It is often the best hour of the day.

**The first ten to fifteen minutes: silence.** Complete. This is when the memory is setting — narratively and chemically — and interrupting it is opening the oven. Anything we say here will be remembered *instead of* what happened.

**Then the car starts talking on its own.** It always does. Our role is not to prompt it. Our role is to not be in the way of it. The single greatest thing our product will ever produce is a conversation we are not part of.

**When does the next destination appear? Not today.** The pull is enormous and it is wrong. Proposing the next place on the way home from the last one converts a completed experience into a queue and quietly teaches the person that our interest in them was transactional all along. Appetite returns in days, not hours — and it returns strongest *after* they have told someone. That is when we speak again.

**What may appear on the way home:** something to hold rather than something to consume. A single line. The answer to the question they left with, if they reach for it. Evidence that the day happened, offered without ceremony.

The most elegant outcome available to us is to have caused the best hour of someone's week and to be completely absent from it.

---

## 11. THE SHARE

People do not tell stories to broadcast. They tell them for three reasons, and only one of them is about the story:

**To prove it happened.** *I was there.*
**To give a gift.** *I found this and thought of you.*
**To relive it.** Narration is how an experience is fixed in place.

The first two are about a relationship with one specific person. That is where our attention belongs.

**When:** inside forty-eight hours, and then again months later when something unrelated triggers it.

**How we help:** we give them **the sentence.** One retellable line per place — the thing a person repeats at a dinner table without meaning to. That single line will do more for this project than any sharing feature ever built, and writing it is among the hardest work we do.

Second: make evidence effortless. Not a curated post — the low-status, high-trust artifact people actually send. One image, one fact, to one person.

**We optimise for one-to-one and never for one-to-many.** A message to a single human being is the most powerful and least measurable form of sharing in existence, which is precisely why no product designs for it.

What we never become: a feed, a follower count, a public profile, a comparison. **The instant a journey can be ranked, it stops being a journey.**

---

## 12. THE SECOND JOURNEY

The second journey does not happen because of anything we send. It happens because the first place **changed the status of the entire country.**

Before: a country. After: a country containing one place they have a relationship with — and a single relationship in a whole country is lonely. It implies others.

The emotional engine is not completion. It is **recalibration.** They now suspect that everywhere they have ever driven past contains something they were not told about. That suspicion is permanent, it is irreversible, and it is the real product. Everything else is delivery.

The second journey feels different from the first, and should: less disbelief, more appetite. They know the shape of the thing now and they want it deliberately. The first was luck. The second is a decision about how they spend their life.

The only thing that causes a second journey is the quality of the first.

---

## 13. THE TENTH JOURNEY

Surprise is a depreciating asset. Relationship is an appreciating one. Over years, the experience must shift its weight from the first to the second.

**What long-term people feel:** fluency. They read a landscape differently now. They pass a smooth rise in a field and think *that is not a hill.* Perception permanently altered — that is the deepest outcome available to anything we build, and it is worth more than any completion.

**And protectiveness.** They become slightly proprietary. They correct people. They are irritated by how a stranger treats a place. This is not possessiveness; it is the first symptom of belonging.

**What we owe them:** not more of the same. Seasons. Weather. The same place in snow, at dawn, in the month it means something. Depth in places they believed they had finished. And above all, a role — because after enough years, taking someone else becomes more satisfying than going again.

**And we owe them honesty about melancholy.** Anyone deep enough into this eventually feels its finite edge. Do not paper over it. The correct answer to that feeling is never *more places.* It is a role.

---

## 14. THE KEEPER

Never a badge. Never a progress state. Never anything on a screen. So when?

**The first time they take someone else, and that person has the moment.**

That is the transition — from receiving to giving. You do not become the keeper of something by accumulating it. You become the keeper of it the first time you successfully hand it to another human being and watch it land.

There is an earlier symptom, and it is protectiveness: the first time they feel something is *theirs* enough to defend it — a place, a story, a fact, the way it should be treated.

**One absolute rule: we may never tell anyone they are a Keeper.** The moment we announce it, it becomes a title. Titles are given, given things are worn, and worn things are not carried. It must be self-diagnosed, privately, probably years in, and probably without words.

Our job is only to build the conditions in which someone can discover it about themselves — and then to say nothing.

---

## 15. THE SILENCE

Silence is our most expensive material. Everything else can be produced. Silence can only be protected.

**The six silences.**

**Arrival silence** — thirty to ninety seconds. Non-negotiable, and the most important in the product.
**The gap** — after the first look, before the first word. It is what makes the first word welcome instead of intrusive.
**The pause** — after every sentence of narration, longer than is comfortable. Meaning happens in the pause, not in the sentence. Writers must build for the gap after the line.
**The moment's silence** — total, absolute, defended against everything.
**The return silence** — the first miles home, while the memory sets.
**The long silence** — the days, weeks, sometimes months in which we say nothing at all. A product that contacts someone every week is a product with nothing to say. Being quiet for a month is what makes the next thing we say matter.

**No music at a place. Ever.** Music is the sound of somebody else's interpretation, and it will overwrite the sound of the wind — which is the thing they will still have in ten years. Music may live in transit and in memory. Never on the ground.

**Silence must never be mistaken for failure.** The hardest craft problem in this entire product is making a person feel the difference between *nothing is happening because that is correct* and *nothing is happening because it is broken.* Everyone will be tempted to solve it by adding something. Solve it another way.

When in doubt: say nothing. Nobody in the history of travel has ever remembered a place for what an application said there.

---

## 16. THE MEMORY

Ten years on.

**What they should remember:** the wind. The temperature. The sound, or the absence of it. The light at the hour they happened to be there. Who was with them. What was said in the car. One fact. And the way it looked in the first second, before anyone spoke.

**What they should forget:** us. The name of it. That there was a screen. That anything was designed.

**Why:** memory is stored in sensation and relationship, not in information. Anything that arrives mediated gets filed under *media* and decays like media. Anything that arrives through the body gets filed under *life* and persists for decades.

The hierarchy, stated plainly:

**If they remember the interface — we failed.** We inserted ourselves into a moment that was not ours.
**If they remember the story — we partly succeeded.** The story was still ours. It is the best kind of mediation, but it is mediation.
**If they remember the wind, the light, the silence, the cave, their child laughing — we succeeded completely.** Those belong to the person and to the place. We caused them and appear nowhere in them.

Which yields the strangest and most important sentence in this document:

> **Our highest achievement is to be forgotten by someone whose life we changed.**

Every instinct to make ourselves memorable is a proposal to trade their memory for ours. Refuse it every time.

And one practical test of a successful memory: ten years later, they can take someone there themselves. They remember the road.

## 17. THE EXPERIENCE LAWS

**On attention**
1. The product speaks less as the place speaks more.
2. Every narration earns a silence longer than feels comfortable.
3. A first look is never narrated.
4. Nothing mediates a first look.
5. Curiosity peaks before explanation — explain after the peak, or not at all.
6. Boredom at a great place is the beginning of attention.
7. Attention is the material. Everything else is packaging.
8. Nothing competes with the real world.
9. Eyes on the screen more than on the place means the visit was replaced.
10. The place always gets the final word.

**On arrival**
11. Nobody feels awe at second five. They feel awkward. Design for the awkwardness.
12. Arrival is a threshold, not an achievement, and is never rewarded.
13. The first thing said on site is specific, not beautiful.
14. Confirm, then withdraw.
15. Technology arrives last, never first.
16. The threshold belongs to the person.

**On the moment**
17. Every place has one moment. Know its exact spot and direction.
18. Never announce a moment in advance.
19. Moments are built out of silence, not sound.
20. Nothing interrupts awe. Nothing.
21. The moment outranks every other need this organisation has.
22. A moment we record is a moment we took.

**On rhythm**
23. Fast to astonish. Slow to reveal.
24. Surprise once per place. Twice is a trick.
25. Urgency must belong to the world — light, season, closing time — or it is manipulation.
26. Sacred holds for ninety seconds before it becomes theatre.
27. Playful never stands near a grave.
28. The experience breathes: expansion, then contraction, never a constant.

**On the journey out**
29. The drive is part of the experience.
30. The passenger is a user.
31. Give the car something to argue about, not something to listen to.
32. Story belongs to the ground. The car gets questions.
33. The closer they get, the less we say.
34. At the last kilometre, only reassurance.
35. Doubt at the threshold turns more cars around than distance ever has.
36. The memory starts before arrival.

**On the return**
37. The first miles home are silent.
38. Never propose the next place on the way back from the last one.
39. The best hour of someone's week may be one we are entirely absent from.
40. Appetite returns in days. Ask then.

**On sharing and becoming**
41. Give them a sentence they can repeat at a table.
42. One person told is worth more than a hundred shown.
43. A journey that can be ranked has stopped being a journey.
44. Nobody is ever told they are a Keeper.
45. Protectiveness is the first symptom of belonging.
46. A Keeper is born the first time they hand it to someone else.

**On memory**
47. If they remember the interface, we failed.
48. If they remember the wind, we succeeded.
49. Our highest achievement is to be forgotten by someone whose life we changed.
50. Design every moment as though it will be remembered without us in it.

## 18. THE EXPERIENCE TEST

Before anything ships, answer one question: **what does the person feel?**

If the answer begins *"they tap,"* *"they click,"* *"they navigate,"* *"they select,"* *"they see a screen with"* — you have described a mechanism, not an experience. Rewrite it.

If the answer begins *"they wonder,"* *"they stop talking,"* *"they look up,"* *"they turn around,"* *"they nudge the person next to them,"* *"they decide,"* *"they remember"* — you are finally designing experiences.

Four diagnostics when the answer is uncertain:

**What is their face doing?** If you cannot picture it, you have not designed it.
**What do they say out loud, and to whom?** Experiences produce speech. Interfaces produce silence of the wrong kind.
**Where are their eyes at the single most important second?** If the answer is the screen, move it.
**Remove us from the sentence entirely — is there still an experience?** If not, we have built something that only exists while being used, and that is the definition of forgettable.

And the last test, which settles almost everything:

> **Does this make someone want to use it — or want to go?**

If a reader of this document finishes it thinking *I want to use that app*, we have written it badly.

They should finish it thinking:

> **I want to go there.**
